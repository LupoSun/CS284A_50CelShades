Summary

Celluloid is a from-scratch cel-shading renderer that unifies three classically separate stylization stages — silhouette extraction, quantized lighting, and stylized texture/palette mapping — into a single interactive pipeline. Our goal is a configurable real-time system that lets users dial in a hand-drawn look across dynamic scenes, with optional procedural in-betweening to push the effect toward animated-film aesthetics.

Problem Description

Context

Cel shading (or toon shading) is a non-photorealistic rendering style that makes 3D geometry read as flat and hand-drawn. A convincing cel look typically depends on three components working in concert: edge/outline rendering for silhouette clarity, quantized shading for the characteristic flat light/shadow bands, and texture/palette mapping for material identity and artistic tone. In practice these are often treated as independent hacks bolted onto a standard shading pass, which makes the style fragile under moving cameras, overlapping geometry, and multi-light scenes.

Problem

We want to treat these three stages as one coherent pipeline and ask: what does it take to get a stable, real-time, configurable cel-shaded look on a non-trivial scene? The hard parts are (1) getting outlines that remain crisp and stable as geometry moves, (2) designing a shader whose discretization of light direction actually reads as an art style rather than as banding artifacts, and (3) keeping all of this fast enough for interactive camera motion. A successful solution opens up low-cost stylized rendering for student games, shorts, and prototyping — contexts where full hand-painted art is out of reach.

General Approach

  • Edges: render expanded back-faces as a black silhouette behind normally shaded front-faces, with screen-space normal/depth discontinuities as a fallback for interior creases.
  • Shading: replace smooth Lambert/Blinn–Phong falloff with a 1D ramp texture lookup, giving sharp light/mid/shadow bands whose thresholds and colors are user-editable.
  • Palette / texture: quantize material textures into a small palette and composite them with the shading bands so texture detail survives the flattening.
  • Motion (stretch): a procedural spring-dynamics layer that adds squash-and-stretch in-betweens to moving objects for a more animated feel.

Goals & Deliverables

We will evaluate the system along three axes:

  • Performance: a fixed set of reference scenes with increasing triangle counts and light counts, measuring milliseconds per frame. Target: interactive (≥30 fps) on the baseline scene on a personal laptop.
  • Visual quality: side-by-side comparisons against reference cel-shaded art and against a standard Phong render of the same scene, judged by hand.
  • Robustness: stress scenes with overlapping objects, concave geometry, and multiple colored light sources. The renderer should remain artifact-free (no flickering outlines, no broken bands) under camera motion.

What We Plan to Deliver (Baseline)

  • A from-scratch cel-shading renderer that handles multi-object, multi-light scenes with proper silhouettes, hard-banded diffuse shading, and quantized palette textures.
  • A small gallery of rendered stills demonstrating the three pipeline stages independently and composed.
  • Performance and robustness measurements on our reference scenes.

What We Hope to Deliver (Aspirational)

  • Fully interactive viewer: live camera, moving objects, and raycast-based object picking/dragging.
  • Runtime-configurable style knobs: number of color bands, edge width, palette size, outline color.
  • Stylized shadow modes: crosshatch and dithered shadows as alternative ramp lookups.
  • Spring in-betweening extended to full scenes, producing animation-style squash/stretch on camera cuts.
  • GPU parallelization of the shading and outline passes for real-time performance on denser scenes.

Questions We Hope to Answer

  • How much of the cel-shaded "feel" comes from outlines vs. quantized lighting vs. palette choice?
  • How does outline stability degrade as geometry becomes concave or self-occluding, and which edge-detection strategy is most robust?
  • What is the per-pass cost budget of a real-time cel pipeline, and which stage is the bottleneck?

Schedule

Week 1 — Foundations

Mesh loading, perspective camera, and a baseline forward renderer with solid shapes and naive outlines. Reference scenes and measurement harness assembled.

Week 2 — Edges and Lights

Silhouette shader working on arbitrary meshes; basic lighting system with multiple lights feeding the shading pass. Initial palette quantization on static textures.

Week 3 — Flat Colors and Minimal Deliverable

Ramp-based hard-banded shading integrated with outlines and palette textures. By the end of this week the baseline deliverable should render end-to-end, with edges hardened against the harder test scenes and a single-object spring-dynamics demo landed.

Week 4 — Stretch Features and Polish

Configurable style knobs, stylized shadows, camera/object interaction, and (if time) GPU parallelization. Record final demo video and write up results.

Resources

Computing & Software

Personal laptops and the class machines. We will write the code from scratch in C++/OpenGL (with GLSL for shaders), using CS184 HW3 as a starting point for the renderer scaffolding.
